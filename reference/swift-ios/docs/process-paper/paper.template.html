The Exochronometer Process

A method for marking instants in time by driving an indicator around a circle and reading its coincidence with inscribed geometry.

SubjectExochronometer DocProcess description StatusConfidential draft Prepared2026-07-24
Abstract

A period of time is represented as a circle. A single indicator is placed at a reference point on the circle and driven around it at a constant angular rate, completing one lap in a chosen period T. One or more geometric figures — regular or star polygons — are inscribed in the circle so that their vertices fall at fixed angular positions. The instants at which the traversing indicator coincides with a vertex are detected and time-stamped as marked events, each event carrying the identity of the figure and vertex it struck. The single process yields a family of instruments: choosing T as a civil unit produces a chronometer; choosing T as a calendar-length period produces a calendar of recurring dates. Because every marked instant is a closed-form function of the chosen parameters, all past and future marks are exactly computable.

§ 1 — THE CORE PROCESS

The main function

The whole system is built on one procedure. It takes a period of time and turns it into a set of precisely-timed, individually-identified events. Stated as a sequence of steps:

  1. Take a circle.

    The circle stands for one complete cycle of time. Going once around the circle is the same as letting the whole period elapse. Nothing on the circle is privileged yet — it is a blank loop.

  2. Place an indicator at the top of the circle.

    A single dot is placed at a fixed reference point — the top, twelve o’clock. This is the origin of the cycle: the instant the period begins. Angular position is measured clockwise from here, from 0° back around to 360°.

  3. Choose how long the indicator takes to traverse the circle.

    A single number, the period T, sets the pace: the time for the dot to travel once around. From it the dot’s position at any moment is fixed exactly —

    // angular position of the indicator, degrees clockwise from top
    θ(t) = ((t − t₀) / T) mod 1 × 360°

    t₀ is the instant the dot sat at the top. The choice of T is the one knob that decides what the finished instrument is — a stopwatch, a day-clock, or a year-calendar all differ only in T.

  4. Inscribe a geometry in the circle.

    A regular polygon or star polygon is drawn touching the circle. A shape with n points places n vertices at equal spacing around the rim — vertex i at angle i × 360/n. The geometry therefore cuts the period into n equal slices, and each vertex marks the boundary of a slice.

  5. Mark where in time the indicator hits the peak of the geometry.

    As the dot sweeps past a vertex it is scored by how close it is. The score rises smoothly to a maximum of exactly 1.0 at the instant the dot sits on the vertex — the peak — then falls away. That peak instant is recorded as a marked event, tagged with which shape and which vertex produced it.

    // activation of a vertex; w is the half-width of the window
    a(t) = cos( (Δθ / w) × π/2 )  for |Δθ| ≤ w,  else 0
    // Δθ = angular gap between indicator and vertex; peak a = 1.0 at Δθ = 0

    An n-point shape therefore produces n evenly-spaced peaks per lap: a triangle marks the thirds of the period, a pentagon the fifths, and so on. Each mark is not merely a time but a labelled coincidence.

The catalogue of geometries

Any number of shapes can be inscribed in the same circle. The shape decides how many marks the period carries and where they fall. Regular polygons from three to eight points give integer divisions of the cycle; each is a different rhythm laid over the same span of time.

Star polygons: fractional divisions

The geometry need not be a simple polygon. A star polygon {n/k} is drawn by stepping k vertices at a time, so its single closed path visits all n points over k laps of the circle. This marks the period at a fractional rate (n/k) — a finer, offset grid of instants that the plain polygons cannot express — while using the same coincidence-detection step unchanged.

Why the process matters

  • It is exact and reversible. Every marked instant is a closed-form function of (t₀, T, n, k), so the full schedule of marks can be computed forward into the future or backward into the past without ever running a clock.
  • It is scale-free. The identical procedure works for a period of one minute or one century; only T changes.
  • It composes. Many geometries can share one circle, and many circles (each with its own T) can be nested; a moment when marks on different circles coincide is itself a detectable, meaningful event.
  • Every mark carries an identity. A mark is a labelled coincidence — this shape, this vertex — so it can act as an index to attach or retrieve information, not merely a bare timestamp.
§ 2 — FIRST APPLICATION

The process as a clock

Set the traversal period T to a familiar unit of time and the process becomes a clock directly. With T = one day, the indicator’s angle is the time of day: a quarter turn is 06:00, half a turn is noon, three-quarters is 18:00. No hands, no numerals are strictly required — position on the loop is the reading.

Because the process is scale-free, several circles can run at once, each with its own period, giving a reading at every scale of time simultaneously. Inscribed geometry adds a second layer: a triangle on the day-circle quietly marks the eight-hour instants; a finer shape marks smaller sub-divisions. The clock therefore shows not only where the moment sits in each cycle but which structural instants it is near.

This is the plain reading of the process: a position within a cycle. Everything the instrument does beyond this is a matter of which period is chosen and which geometry is inscribed.

§ 3 — SECOND APPLICATION

The process as a calendar

Now lengthen the period. Set T to a calendar span — one year, one lunar month, one week — and the marked instants stop being times of day and become dates. Each vertex of an inscribed geometry maps to a fixed point within the period:

// the calendar date of vertex i on a period that began at cycleStart
date(i) = cycleStart + (i / n) × T

Inscribe a pentagon in a one-year circle and the year is partitioned into five recurring dates; the traversing indicator is simply “today,” sweeping toward the next of them. Choose a different geometry and the year is divided differently. Because the marks are computable in closed form, the calendar’s recurring dates can be listed for any year, past or future, without observation.

The length of the chosen period is the whole difference between the two applications. A short period reads as a clock; a long period reads as a calendar. The generating process — dot, circle, inscribed geometry, peak detection — is identical in both.

Optional refinement: a shared origin

Where the reference point t₀ — the instant the indicator sits at the top — is tied to an observer-independent astronomical event (for example a solstice or a new moon) rather than to local midnight, every circle shows the same geometry at the same instant for every user everywhere. The marks then form a single shared frame rather than a local one.

§ 4 — GENERALISATION

Further embodiments

  • Any cyclic quantity. The traversed variable need not be wall-clock time; any repeating quantity that can be normalised to one lap of a circle drives the same process.
  • Sound. Mapping each inscribed figure to a frequency proportional to its division count turns the marks into audible events, and figures active at the same instant sound together as a chord — a direct rendering of the moment’s structure.
  • Indexing. Because each mark is a labelled coincidence, it can serve as a key: information attached at a mark is retrievable at every later recurrence of the same phase, turning the geometry into an addressing scheme for time-stamped data.
§ 5 — ILLUSTRATIVE CLAIMS

The process, stated as claims

The following restate the process in claim form to isolate the steps for which protection is sought. They are drafting aids, not filed claims.

  1. A method of marking instants in a period of time, comprising:
    1. representing the period as a circle;
    2. placing an indicator at a reference point on the circle;
    3. advancing the indicator around the circle at a constant rate such that one complete traversal corresponds to a chosen period T;
    4. inscribing at least one geometric figure in the circle, the figure defining a plurality of vertices at fixed angular positions;
    5. detecting each instant at which the advancing indicator coincides with a vertex of the figure; and
    6. recording each such instant as a marked event associated with the identity of the coincident figure and vertex.
  2. The method of claim 1, wherein detecting the coincidence comprises evaluating an activation that rises to a defined maximum as the angular separation between indicator and vertex approaches zero, and recording the time of that maximum as the marked event.
  3. The method of claim 1, wherein the geometric figure is a star polygon {n/k} whose path visits n vertices over k traversals, whereby the period is marked at a fractional rate n/k.
  4. The method of claim 1, wherein a plurality of circles, each having a distinct period T, are advanced concurrently, and a further event is detected when marks on two or more circles coincide.
  5. The method of claim 1, wherein T is a unit of civil time, whereby the angular position of the indicator constitutes a reading of the current time within that unit (a chronometer).
  6. The method of claim 1, wherein T is a calendar period and each vertex maps to a recurring calendar date within the period (a calendar), the dates being computed in closed form for arbitrary periods.
  7. The method of claim 1, wherein the reference point is anchored to an observer-independent astronomical instant, whereby the marked events are identical for all observers.
  8. The method of claim 1, further comprising mapping each figure to a frequency proportional to its vertex count and emitting a tone at each marked event, figures active concurrently forming a chord.
  9. The method of claim 1, further comprising using a marked event as an index to store and later retrieve data at recurrences of the same phase.

All figures are generated directly from the software’s own geometry and timing routines (indicator angle, vertex placement, and the cosine activation window), rendered in the instrument’s native line-art form.
